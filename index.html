01
Introduction to Corporations
Essay
What is a Corporation?
  • Distinct legal entity — can borrow, sue, be sued; not a person but legally treated like one
  • Owned by shareholders — ownership divided into shares; initially managers/backers, then public
  • Separation of ownership and management — shareholders elect board → board appoints managers. Firm has permanence even if managers/shareholders change
  • Limited liability — shareholders not personally responsible for firm's debts. Maximum loss = investment
  • Infinite life — survives changes in ownership
Advantages
  • Easier to raise large amounts of capital
  • Limited liability attracts investors
  • Shares freely transferable
  • Professional management
Disadvantages
  • Complex structure, costly to run
  • Double taxation — corporate tax on profits + personal tax on dividends
  • Moral hazard / agency problems (see Topic 7)
Two Core Questions Corporations Face

1. Investment Decision

What real assets should the firm invest in? (LHS of balance sheet)

2. Financing Decision

How should the firm pay for those assets? Debt, equity, or internal funds? (RHS of balance sheet)

Financial Manager's Role
  • Goal: Maximise shareholder wealth (= maximise stock price)
  • Investing: Correctly compute cash flows; use appropriate discount rate
  • Financing: Identify costs/benefits of instruments; choose optimal mix
  • Dividend decisions: How much to pay out and in what form
  • Risk management: Identify risks and find hedging instruments
Key principle: Invest only if the project earns more than the opportunity cost of capital (hurdle rate)
Other Business Forms (for comparison)
  • Sole Proprietorship: Single owner/manager. Simple but unlimited liability
  • Partnership: Tax advantage (only personal income tax, no double taxation), but unlimited or limited liability
  • Corporations better for raising capital at scale
Financial Markets & Intermediaries

Financial Institutions

  • Transport savings across time — connect savers and borrowers
  • Offer choices: short-term borrowing (banks), long-term (bonds), equity (shares)
  • Provide information: interest rates, market values

Financial Markets

  • Primary: New money raised (IPOs, new bonds)
  • Secondary: Existing securities traded; gives shareholders freedom to manage wealth
  • OTC: No organised exchange
Roles of the Financial System (beyond channelling savings)
  • Borrowing & lending: Allocate savings toward those who can best use them
  • Payment mechanism: Allow individuals to make and receive payments quickly and safely over long distances
  • Pooling risk: Allow individuals and firms to share and diversify risk
  • Information: Reveal the "real" value of securities and commodities; allow estimation of expected returns for investors
Fintech Revolution
Three Key Drivers
  • Big data: Vast volumes of financial consumer data now available
  • AI/ML: New ways to extract information and make predictions from data
  • Cloud computing: Small firms share costs with others — levels the playing field against large incumbents
Key Fintech Developments
  • Digital payments: Cards/mobile replacing cash and cheques
  • P2P lending: Small/medium firms bypass banks to access credit
  • Crowdfunding: Internet-based fundraising for startups
  • AI credit scoring: Uses social media, phone data, online shopping to assess creditworthiness (even without bank account)
  • Cryptocurrencies (e.g. Bitcoin): Pose risks to financial stability and monetary policy (price volatility); central banks exploring digital currencies
02
Investment Decisions
Essay + Calculation
Present Value (PV)

Money today > same money in future because it can be invested to earn a return.

Single cash flow
PV = Ct / (1 + r)t
C_t = cash flow at time t; r = discount rate; t = number of periods

Multiple cash flows (sum each period)
PV = C₁/(1+r) + C₂/(1+r)² + C₃/(1+r)³ + … + C_T/(1+r)^T
Net Present Value (NPV) ⭐ Best Method
If initial cost paid today
NPV = PV of future cash flows − Initial Investment (Q₀)

If costs also spread over time
NPV = Σ C_t/(1+r_t)^t − Q₀ − Σ Q_t/(1+r_t)^t
Decision Rule: Accept if NPV > 0 (creates value). Reject if NPV < 0.
5 Key Advantages of NPV
  • Clear benchmark: NPV > 0 = accept
  • Uses all cash flows over project life
  • Accounts for time value of money (discounting)
  • Ignores irrelevant accounting numbers
  • Value additivity: NPVs of different projects can be added together
Rate of Return (RoR)
RoR = (C − Q) / Q = Profit / Investment
C = cash inflow; Q = cost of investment

Rule: Accept if RoR > Opportunity Cost of Capital (OCC).

⚠ Gives same accept/reject as NPV but NOT quantitatively equivalent. A small project may have high RoR but tiny NPV.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR) — Second Best
IRR: the rate that sets NPV = 0 (the "break-even" discount rate)
0 = Σ C_t/(1+IRR)^t − Q

Rule: Accept if IRR > Opportunity Cost of Capital.

Intuition: If the project's internal return exceeds what you could earn elsewhere at the same risk, it's worth doing.

How to Calculate IRR
  • NPV Profile method: NPV is a steadily declining function of the discount rate — as r rises, NPV falls. Plot NPV at several discount rates; the point where the curve crosses zero (NPV = 0) is the IRR
  • Trial and error: Try different discount rates, interpolate between a positive and negative NPV to find where NPV = 0
  • Spreadsheet/calculator: Most accurate method in practice — use IRR function directly
NPV Profile visual idea: Imagine a downward-sloping curve. x-axis = discount rate, y-axis = NPV. Where it crosses the x-axis = IRR. If the OCC is to the left of the IRR (i.e. OCC < IRR) → NPV is positive → accept. If OCC is to the right → NPV negative → reject.
Pitfalls of IRR
  • Borrowing reversal: For projects that begin with positive inflows (borrowing), rule reverses: accept if OCC > IRR
  • Multiple/no IRRs: If cash flow signs change more than once, there can be multiple IRRs (# IRRs = # sign changes). Rule breaks down entirely
  • Multiple opportunity costs: One IRR cannot be compared against several discount rates across different periods
  • Mutually exclusive projects: A smaller project may have higher IRR but lower NPV — IRR misleads on scale. Always use NPV to choose between mutually exclusive options
Payback Period

How many years until initial investment is paid back from cash flows.

Count cumulative cash flows until they equal initial investment

Rule: Accept if payback < cut-off period (e.g. 2 or 3 years).

Pros

  • Simple to calculate
  • Useful for liquidity screening
  • 64% of large companies use it

    • Cons

  • No clear benchmark for cut-off
  • Ignores all cash flows after cut-off
  • Ignores time value of money
    • Accounting (Book) Rate of Return (ARR / BRR)
    ARR = Average Profits / Average Assets (Book Value)

    Rule: Accept if project's ARR > company's current ARR.

    Pros

  • Uses readily available accounting data

    • Cons

  • No correct benchmark — past profitability ≠ right hurdle
  • Ignores time value of money
  • Based on profits not cash flows
    • Profitability Index (PI) — Capital Rationing
    PI = NPV / Initial Investment (Q)

    Rule: Rank by PI; pick highest NPV per £ invested. Only useful under capital rationing (limited budget).

    • Breaks down with multiple constraints
    • Breaks down when projects are interdependent
    Method Ranking Summary
    MethodVerdictWhy
    NPV✓ BestAll cash flows, time value, clear rule, additive
    IRR✓ 2nd BestSame answer as NPV if used carefully; watch pitfalls
    PI⚠ SituationalOnly for capital rationing
    Payback⚠ SituationalSimple but ignores long-term value
    ARR/BRR✗ AvoidDoesn't use cash flows or time value of money
    Specimen Exam — Payback Periods

    Projects (£): A: −2000, +500, +500, +1000, 0 | B: −500, 0, +500, +1000, +2000 | C: −3000, +1000, +1000, +1000, +3000

    Payback: A = 3 yrs (500+500+1000=2000) | B = 2 yrs (500+1000=1500 wait... 0+500+1000≥500 → year 3? No: cumulative: Y1=0, Y2=500, Y3=1500 ≥ 500 → 2 yrs) | C = 3 yrs

    Correct: A = 3 yrs, B = 2 yrs, C = 3 yrs

    Cut-off 2 yrs: Accept B only. Cut-off 3 yrs: Accept A, B, C.

    NPV @ 10%: B and C positive. NPV(B) = £2,030.56. NPV(C) = £1,535.89. NPV(A) < 0.

    Opportunity Cost of Capital: The return foregone by choosing this project over the next best alternative of equal risk. It is the discount rate r. Depends on project risk, NOT company's own risk. Example: investing in property instead of bonds → the bond interest rate is your OCC.
    Capital Budgeting

    The decision-making process for whether to accept or reject investment proposals. All the methods above (NPV, IRR, Payback, ARR, PI) are capital budgeting tools.

    • Companies invest in real assets — tangible (factories, machinery, inventory) or intangible (patents, brands)
    • You pay now; returns come in the future → need to express future cash flows in today's terms via discounting
    • Primary goal: assets should be worth more than they cost
    PI — Choosing Best Combination Under Capital Rationing

    When budget is limited, rank projects by PI and pick the combination that maximises total NPV per £ invested.

    Logic Example

    Comparing B+C vs A+D: B+C gives higher total NPV AND higher PIs than A+D.

    A+D may have higher combined absolute NPV but lower NPV per £ invested.

    Under capital rationing → choose B+C (highest "bang per buck").

    PI breaks down when projects are interdependent or constraints exist in multiple periods — revert to NPV directly in those cases.

    03
    Corporate Financing
    Essay + Calculation
    Sources of Finance

    Internal Funds

    • Retained earnings (profits reinvested)
    • Depreciation charges
    • Accounts for over 2/3 of corporate financing in US, UK, Germany, Japan

    External Funds

    • Debt: Bank loans, bonds
    • New equity: Issuing new shares
    Why Firms Prefer Internal Funding — and Its Downside

    Why preferred

  • Avoids costs of issuing new securities or negotiating debt
  • Shareholders are happy — retained profits funding positive NPV projects means they forego dividends but stock gains in market value
  • No dilution of ownership or control

    • Downside

  • Managers too averse to external funding may over-rely on internal funds
  • Can cause them to miss risky but positive-NPV projects (links to agency problem)
  • No external scrutiny on how funds are used

    • How do corporations pay for real assets? By selling claims on those real assets and the cash flows they generate — essentially sharing income. This can be via securities (traded in financial markets: bonds, shares) or financial assets (held but not traded: bank loans requiring interest).

    What is Common Equity?
    • Corporation owned by its common stockholders
    • Companies raise new cash by issuing new shares
    • Issued and outstanding: shares currently held by investors (count in EPS calculations)
    • Issued but not outstanding (Treasury shares): shares bought back from investors; held by the company itself; no votes, no dividends
    Book Value vs Market Value
    Book value — what an asset originally cost (historical accounting value)
    Market value — what an asset is worth today (current price)
    Market values are often much larger than book values → the market debt ratio is often much lower than the book debt ratio. This matters when assessing how much debt a company actually has relative to its true value. Always use market values for MM and WACC calculations.
    Preferred Equity (vs Common Equity)
    • Preferred dividends: Fixed rate set at time of issue; paid before common stockholders
    • Priority: Common shareholders receive nothing until preferred dividends are paid in full
    • Control rights: No voting rights under normal conditions. Only gain some voting rights if the company fails to pay the preferred dividend
    • Cumulative: Most preferred stock is cumulative — missed dividends accumulate and must be fully paid before any common dividends can resume
    • Sits between debt (senior) and common equity (junior) in the capital structure
    Cash Flow Rights of Equity (Common Shareholders)
    • Residual claimants: Get whatever is left after all other claims paid
    • Unlevered firm: 100% cash flow and control rights go to equity
    • Levered firm: Residual cash flows only; lenders (banks, bondholders) paid first
    • Shareholders vote in proportion to shares held (normally 1 share = 1 vote)
    Control Rights of Common Equity (Exam Essay Topic)
    • Shareholders have ultimate control over investment decisions, mergers, recruitment
    • Control limited to entitlement to vote at AGM and on major decisions
    • In widely held corporations, individual shareholders have limited power (few shares = little voting power)
    • Simple majority needed for most decisions
    • Supermajority (e.g. 75%+) needed for major decisions like mergers
    • Control rights extinguished if firm goes bankrupt — control passes to creditors
    • Classes of shares: e.g. Meta: Class A (public, 1 vote) vs Class B (founders, 10 votes) — same cash flow rights, different control. Premium for superior voting stock.
    Cash Flow Rights of Debt
    • Firm promises regular interest payments + repayment of principal
    • Senior but limited: paid before shareholders, but only entitled to agreed interest/principal (no share in upside)
    • Default: if firm can't pay, lenders may seize assets (secured debt) and trigger bankruptcy
    Control Rights of Debt
    • No voting rights so long as financial obligations are met
    • Debt covenants restrict management actions (limiting extra debt, restricting dividend payments)
    • If company goes bankrupt, debt holders gain control
    Debt: Financial Manager's Decisions
    DecisionOptionsKey Consideration
    MaturityShort vs Long termMatch to time horizon of project
    Interest rateFixed vs FloatingBanks usually float; bonds usually fixed coupons
    CurrencyDomestic vs ForeignMatch to where cash flows generated
    PrioritySenior vs JuniorJunior (also called subordinated) debt paid after senior; riskier = higher return demanded
    SecuritySecured vs UnsecuredCollateral protects lender; firm can seize assets on default
    ConvertibilityStraight vs ConvertibleConvertible bonds can be swapped for a predetermined number of shares if price rises (no obligation to convert). Warrant: gives owner the right to purchase a set number of shares at a set price before a set date — similar logic, but separate from the bond
    Debt Ratio
    Debt Ratio = Long-term Debt / (Long-term Debt + Equity)
    Note: market values often >> book values, so market debt ratio often lower than book ratio
    Worked Example — Debt Ratio (From Flashcard)

    Executive Clark: 25m shares @ $10 = $250m equity. Issues $160m debt, repurchases stock.

    Shares repurchased = $160m / $10 = 16 million

    Remaining shares = 25m − 16m = 9m → Equity value = 9m × $10 = $90m

    Market value of firm = $160m + $90m = $250m (unchanged — MM applies)

    Debt ratio = $160m / $250m = 0.64

    Who gains/loses? No one — MM Proposition I applies

    04
    Equity Financing
    Essay + Calculation
    1. Venture Capital (VC)
    • Definition: Private equity investment in new, private companies
    • VCs pool funds from pension funds, mutual funds, wealthy individuals
    • Seek promising startups, finance in exchange for large share of stock
    • Not passive: take board seats, monitor closely, advise, recruit senior management, help bring products to market faster
    • Corporate ventures: Established companies also sometimes invest in new innovative firms — called corporate ventures. Same concept but backed by an existing company rather than a VC fund
    VC Firm Structure
    RoleWhoResponsibilities & Pay
    General PartnerVC management companyMakes and oversees investments. Paid: fixed management fee (~2%) + carried interest (~20% of profits above a hurdle). Carried interest = their share of the upside — aligns GP incentives with LP returns
    Limited PartnersPension funds, mutual funds, wealthy individualsProvide the capital; passive investors; receive ~80% of profits
    • Most VC funds are limited private partnerships with a fixed life of ~10 years
    • Multiple VC partnerships often grouped together — providing funds to distressed companies, buying out whole companies and taking them private = private equity investing

    Funding Stages

    1
    Seed funding: Angel investors; very first external capital; personal savings/family also common. A solid business plan is essential (product, target market, vision, milestones)
    2
    Series A: After hitting initial checkpoints; staff, marketing, scaling. Often involves convertible preferred shares (can convert to ordinary shares). May include liquidation preference — VC gets paid first if company is wound up. Risks: down round (new shares at lower value, diluting early investors); refusal of further VC funding sends a bad signal to other investors — implies the VC who knows the firm best doesn't believe in it
    3
    Series B / Mezzanine: Full-scale production; hybrid debt/equity. Mezzanine sits between debt (senior) and equity (junior) in the capital structure — riskier than debt but safer than common equity
    VC Exit Routes
    Trade Sale — sell stake to larger firm
    IPO — firm goes public; VC sells on market
    VC Rules for Success
    • Accept high uncertainty if there's even a small chance of massive success — big wins compensate for many losses (portfolio logic)
    • Identify failures early and cut losses — don't throw good money after bad
    Why IPOs Are Attractive for Everyone
    • VCs can sell stock and realise their returns
    • Entrepreneurs can retain control of the company (if they retain enough shares)
    • Stock can be freely traded in the capital market — provides liquidity
    • For the VC market to work well, an active stock exchange specialising in young/growing firms is needed (e.g. Nasdaq in the US)
    2. Initial Public Offerings (IPOs)

    Primary Offering

    New shares sold → firm raises fresh cash

    Secondary Offering

    Existing shareholders sell → firm receives no new cash

    Most IPOs are a mix of both.

    Why Go Public?
    • Access larger pool of capital
    • Enhanced public profile and credibility
    • Use shares as acquisition currency
    • Exit route for VCs and angel investors
    • Allow employees to realise share option value
    IPO Process (Step by Step)
    1
    Select Underwriter — investment bank advises, buys issue from company, resells to public
    2
    Registration Statement — filed with regulator (SEC in US; FCA in UK); firm history, proposed projects, regulatory compliance
    3
    Prospectus — formal public summary of registration statement; what investors read
    4
    Book Building & Road Show — company + underwriters tour institutional investors; gauge demand and price
    5
    Final Pricing — use book-building data to set issue price
    IPO Costs (Exam Essay Topic)

    1. The Spread

    Spread = Shares sold × (Offering price − Price paid to company)
    E.g.: 3m shares, company gets £40, public pays £43 → Spread = 3m × £3 = £9m

    Underwriter's compensation for taking on resale risk.

    2. Administrative Costs

    Legal counsel, financial advisors, accountants, FCA/SEC registration fees, printing, management time.

    3. Underpricing (Hidden / Implicit Cost) ⭐

    Cost of Underpricing = Shares sold × (End-of-day market price − IPO price)

    "Money left on the table" — value the company could have raised but didn't.

    Why Do Firms Underprice?
    • Improve post-IPO performance: High first-day returns attract future investors
    • Winner's Curse: If you easily get all shares you wanted → nobody else wanted them → suggests you overpaid. Firms underprice to attract smart investors
    • Agency Problem: Underpricing benefits underwriter (less risk, can reward favoured clients) at firm's expense
    eBay IPO Example

    Issue price: $18. Close day 1: $47.375. Massive underpricing → huge "money left on table."

    3. Subsequent (Seasoned) Issues

    General Cash Offer

    Sale of new securities open to all investors. Similar to IPO procedure. Seasoned offering = firm already public.

    Shelf Registration: File one registration covering several future issues (up to 3 years) — saves admin cost for repeat issuers.

    Rights Issue (Privileged Subscription)

    New shares offered first to existing shareholders at a discount — protects them from dilution.

    Shareholder can: (1) Accept, (2) Sell the right, (3) Do nothing (diluted).

    Rights Issue Calculation ⭐ (Highly Examinable)
    Step 1: Ex-rights price — what each share is worth after the new shares dilute the value
    Ex-rights price = (N × P_old + n × P_issue) / (N + n)
    N = number of old shares held. P_old = current (rights-on) share price. n = new shares you're entitled to buy. P_issue = discounted subscription (issue) price. N + n = total shares after.
    Plain English: total value of your holding after divided by total shares = new price per share. It falls because new shares were issued cheap.

    Step 2: Value of a right (rights-on) — if you still own the right (haven't exercised or sold yet)
    Value of right (rights-on) = (P_old − P_issue) / (N/n + 1)
    N/n = how many existing shares give you 1 new share. +1 because after buying 1 new share, you hold (N/n + 1) shares total. Plain English: discount available per new share, spread across all shares that "earn" one right.

    Step 3: Value of a right (ex-rights) — after the shares go ex-rights
    Value of right (ex-rights) = (P_ex − P_issue) / (N/n)
    P_ex = ex-rights price from Step 1. N/n = rights needed per new share. Plain English: the gain from buying at the discount price vs the current ex-rights market price, per right held.

    Note: Value of right (rights-on) = Value of right (ex-rights) always — they must be equal by no-arbitrage.
    Worked Example — Coffee-Tes Corp

    Current price: £24.73. Issue price: £13.93. Terms: 3 new for every 20 held.

    Value of 20 existing shares: 20 × £24.73 = £494.60

    New shares purchased: 3 × £13.93 = £41.79

    Total value after: £494.60 + £41.79 = £536.39

    Total shares: 20 + 3 = 23

    Ex-rights price = £536.39 / 23 = £23.32

    Value of each right = £24.73 − £23.32 = £1.41

    Ex-rights price lower than original because new shares issued at discount — value diluted across more shares. Right worth £1.41 = privilege of buying at £13.93 what's now worth £23.32.

    Past Exam — Paper Ink Company (2019)

    Rights issue at €4/share, 1 new for every 4 held. 10m shares outstanding, share price €6.

    (a) Total new money: New shares = 10m / 4 = 2.5m. New money = 2.5m × €4 = €10m

    (b) Value of a right:

    4 shares × €6 = €24 + 1 × €4 = €4 → Total = €28 / 5 shares = €5.60 ex-rights price

    Value of right = €6 − €5.60 = €0.40

    Alternative Issue Procedures
    • Direct Share Listing: Register directly on exchange; bypass IPO; existing shareholders sell; cannot issue new shares this way
    • Open Auction: Investors submit bids; sold to highest bidders
    • Green Shoe Option: Allows underwriters to buy extra stock to stabilise price if demand high
    • Best-Efforts: Underwriter sells as much as possible; no guarantee
    • All-or-None: Entire issue sells at offering price or deal cancelled
    05
    Debt Policy (Capital Structure)
    Essay + Calculation
    Key Definitions
    • Capital structure: A firm's mix of debt and equity financing
    • Financial leverage / gearing: Financing investment partly or wholly through debt
    • Firm Value: V = D + E (Debt + Equity)
    Return on assets — what the whole firm earns
    r_A = Expected Operating Income / (D + E)
    D + E = total market value of firm (debt + equity). r_A is the return earned on ALL the firm's assets regardless of how they're financed

    r_A as weighted average of debt and equity returns
    r_A = [E/(E+D)] × r_E + [D/(E+D)] × r_D
    E/(E+D) = equity's share of total firm value (weight). D/(E+D) = debt's share. r_E = return equity holders demand. r_D = return debt holders demand. This is also WACC before tax.
    Plain English: total firm return = equity's weight × equity's return + debt's weight × debt's return
    Modigliani-Miller Framework (No Taxes) ⭐ Central Exam Topic
    Core idea: In a perfect capital market, firm value is determined by its real assets — not by how it is financed. Capital structure is irrelevant.
    MM Assumptions
    AssumptionReality
    Efficient markets (no arbitrage)Information is imperfect and asymmetric
    No taxationInterest IS tax-deductible — creates advantage to debt
    No bankruptcy costsDefaulting is costly — legal fees, lost customers
    Investment unaffected by financingFinancial distress can limit investment

    MM's framework is powerful because it tells us WHERE to look for value when assumptions are relaxed.

    MM Proposition I (No Taxes)
    Statement: The market value of any firm is independent of its capital structure: VU = VL
    Proof via Law of One Price
    • Buy 1% of Unlevered firm U: invest 0.01×VU, get 0.01×Profits
    • Buy 1% of both debt AND equity of Levered firm L: invest 0.01×(D+E) = 0.01×VL, get 0.01×Profits
    • Same return → same price → VU = VL
    Key Implications
    • Firm value determined by real assets (LHS of balance sheet), not financing mix (RHS)
    • Law of conservation of value: You cannot create value simply by repackaging the same cash flows differently. Splitting income between debt and equity holders doesn't change total income — just like cutting a pizza into more slices doesn't make it bigger
    • Applies equally to preferred vs common equity, short vs long-term debt, any combination
    MM Proposition II (No Taxes)
    Statement: As leverage (D/E) increases, equity holders demand a higher return — exactly offsetting cheaper debt. WACC remains constant.
    MM Prop II — Cost of Equity rises with leverage
    r_E = r_A + (r_A − r_D) × D/E
    r_A = what all assets earn (fixed by real investments). r_D = cost of debt (what lenders charge). D/E = how much debt per £1 of equity.
    Plain English: equity return = asset return + a risk premium. The premium grows with D/E. The spread (r_A − r_D) is how much riskier equity is vs debt — multiplied by the leverage ratio.
    If r_A = 15%, r_D = 5%, D/E = 0.5 → r_E = 15% + (15%−5%) × 0.5 = 15% + 5% = 20%

    WACC (no taxes) — always equals r_A regardless of leverage
    WACC = [E/(E+D)] × r_E + [D/(E+D)] × r_D = r_A
    Even though r_E rises with leverage, the cheaper debt and smaller equity weight exactly cancel it out. WACC stays constant = r_A.
    Intuition
    • More debt → equity is riskier (residual claimants face more volatile earnings)
    • Equity holders demand higher r_E to compensate
    • Higher r_E exactly offsets the cheaper debt → WACC unchanged

    ⚠ As D/E rises further: risk of default increases → bondholders also demand higher r_D → r_E rises less steeply than formula suggests

    Effect of Leverage on EPS
    EPS (unlevered) = Operating Income / Shares outstanding
    EPS (levered) = (Operating Income − Interest) / Shares outstanding
    • Leverage increases EPS and expected return when operating income is above the break-even level
    • Leverage decreases EPS and returns when operating income is below the break-even level
    • At the break-even level of operating income, EPS is the same whether levered or not
    • Leverage also changes cash flow allocation — some flows to debt holders, remainder to equity
    • Higher risk for equity holders → they demand a higher return
    • Key point: Leverage increases EPS through increased risk, NOT through value creation (MM)
    Leverage & EPS — Flashcard Worked Example

    Firm: 1,000 shares @ £10, market value = £10,000. No debt initially.

    Levered version: borrows £5,000 @ 10% interest, buys back 500 shares @ £10.

    After leverage: 500 shares remaining, debt = £5,000, equity = £5,000. Annual interest = £500.

    All-EquityDebt + Equity
    Expected EPS (£)1.502.00
    Price per share (£)1010
    Expected return on shares15%20%

    When operating income > £1,000 (break-even): levered EPS > unlevered — leverage helps equity holders.

    When operating income < £1,000: levered EPS < unlevered — leverage hurts equity holders.

    The share price stays at £10 in both cases — higher EPS in levered firm exactly reflects higher risk. No value created.

    What Happens as Leverage Increases Further?
    • As D/E rises → risk of default increases → bondholders also demand a higher r_D
    • This means r_E rises less rapidly than the MM Prop II formula predicts
    • Why? Because as debt becomes riskier, some of the firm's risk is transferred from equity holders to bondholders (they now share some of the downside)
    • The slope of the r_E line therefore decreases as D/E increases — flattens out at high leverage
    MM Prop II formula r_E = r_A + (r_A − r_D) × D/E assumes r_D is constant. In practice, once firms become highly leveraged, r_D rises too, and the formula overstates how fast r_E climbs.
    Breadth of MM Proposition I

    MM Prop I applies broadly — not just to the basic debt vs equity choice. It applies equally to:

    • Choices between different types of preferred vs common equity
    • Choices between short-term vs long-term debt
    • Any combination of securities whatsoever
    In a perfect market: any combination of securities is as good as another. The financial manager cannot create value through clever financing alone — value comes from real assets and investment decisions.
    With Corporate Taxes: Tax Shield on Debt
    Key insight: Interest is tax-deductible (paid on pre-tax income). Dividends are NOT (paid on after-tax income). This gives debt a tax advantage — a "disguised tax subsidy."
    Annual Tax Shield
    Tax Shield = T_C × r_D × D
    T_C = corporate tax rate; r_D = interest rate; D = debt outstanding

    WACC after tax (debt has lower effective cost)
    WACC_after-tax = [E/(E+D)] × r_E + [D/(E+D)] × r_D × (1 − T_C)
    Tax Shield Example

    Debt = £1m @ 8%. T_C = 25%.

    Interest payment = £80,000/yr

    Tax Shield = 25% × £80,000 = £20,000 saved per year

    Implication with Taxes
    • Under MM (no taxes): capital structure irrelevant
    • With corporate taxes: more debt → lower WACC → higher firm value
    • Suggests firms should maximise debt — but this ignores bankruptcy costs
    MM No-Tax Worked Example — Bread Baking Company ⭐

    All-equity: 100,000 shares @ £5. r_equity = 15%. Issues £100,000 debt @ 5% to repurchase stock. No taxes. Shares represent a perpetuity.

    (i) WACC before debt issued

    All-equity → WACC = r_equity = 15%

    For an all-equity firm, WACC = r_A = r_equity by definition.

    (ii) Operating income & EPS before leverage

    Market value = 100,000 × £5 = £500,000

    r_A = Operating income / Market value → Operating income = 0.15 × £500,000 = £75,000

    All income goes to equity holders → EPS = £75,000 / 100,000 = £0.75

    (iii) After issuing £100,000 debt — new r_equity and WACC

    Shares repurchased = £100,000 / £5 = 20,000 → 80,000 shares left

    D = £100,000; E = £500,000 − £100,000 = £400,000

    Interest = 0.05 × £100,000 = £5,000

    Income to shareholders = £75,000 − £5,000 = £70,000

    EPS = £70,000 / 80,000 = £0.875

    Shares = perpetuity → r_equity = EPS / Price = £0.875 / £5 = 17.5%

    WACC = 0.05 × (100k/500k) + 0.175 × (400k/500k) = 0.01 + 0.14 = 15% — unchanged!

    MM Prop I & II confirmed: capital structure doesn't change firm value or WACC. Higher r_E exactly offsets cheaper debt.

    Agency Problems, Compensation & Performance
    Essay
    The Principal-Agent Problem
    Principal = Shareholders (owners)
    Agent = Managers (run the firm)
    Problem: Managers have asymmetric information (know more than shareholders) and may pursue their own interests rather than maximising shareholder wealth.
    5 Forms of Agency Problems (When managers get fixed salary)

    1. Reduced Effort

    • Low incentive to seek out truly valuable (positive NPV) projects
    • Errors of omission (failure to act) can be as damaging as errors of commission

    2. Perks (Consumption of Private Benefits)

    • Managers extract non-cash benefits at firm's expense
    • Company cars, lavish offices, luxury entertainment, excessive travel
    • Directly reduces value available to shareholders

    3. Overinvestment (Free Cash Flow Problem)

    • Empire building: Managers prefer larger firms (more prestige/power) → invest in zero/negative NPV projects just to grow
    • Entrenching investment: Invest in projects specifically requiring manager's own skills → makes them harder to replace

    4. Risk-Taking Problems

    • Gambling for resurrection: Struggling firm managers take excessive risks hoping big wins save them
    • Risk aversion: Managers avoid risky but positive-NPV projects because they fear personal consequences of failure (unlike shareholders who are diversified)

    5. Short-Termism

    • Actions that boost short-run results at expense of long-run value
    • Cutting R&D, maintenance spending to hit this quarter's target
    • Especially problematic when tenure is short or bonuses tied to near-term metrics
    Agency costs = firm's potential value not fully achieved + shareholders must spend money monitoring.
    Solutions to the Agency Problem

    Solution 1: Monitoring

    MonitorRole
    Board of DirectorsOversee management, set pay, hire/fire CEO
    ShareholdersVote on decisions; activist shareholders pressure management
    AuditorsVerify financial statements are accurate
    LendersDebt covenants restrict risky behaviour and excess dividends
    Takeover threatPoorly managed firm may be acquired; disciplines behaviour
    • Governance system: "comply or explain" principle
    • Limitations: costly; diminishing returns; managers always know more than monitors (information asymmetry)

    Solution 2: Management Compensation

    Design challenge: attract capable managers (participation constraint — package must be competitive) AND give right incentives (incentive constraint — must align with shareholder interests).

    BasisDescriptionProblem
    Input-based (effort)Pay managers based on how hard they workEffort is very difficult to observe and measure — managers can appear busy without creating value
    Output-based (results)Pay managers based on measurable outcomes (profits, share price, value added)✓ The practical solution — focus on what managers actually produce
    Conclusion: Compensation must be based on output, not input. Since effort cannot be reliably monitored, you reward results instead — which is what stock-price-linked compensation achieves.

    Solution 3: Linking Pay to Stock Price ⭐ (Exam Essay Topic)

    Why stock price? Capital markets continuously price in information about firm performance → automatic, low-cost monitor of managerial performance.
    TypeHow It WorksKey Feature
    Stock OptionsRight (not obligation) to buy shares at fixed exercise price in futureProfit only if share price rises above exercise price — strong upside incentive
    Restricted StockShares granted but must be held for several years before sellingDiscourages short-termism — managers care about long-run
    Performance SharesShares awarded only if firm meets specific targets vs. industry peersFilters market-wide movements — rewards outperformance, not just bull market gains
    Why Stock-Price Compensation Helps
    • Aligns manager and shareholder incentives directly
    • Reduces need for costly external monitoring
    • Managers share in value they create
    Why It's Still Imperfect
    • Short-termism persists: Cutting investment/R&D to inflate near-term stock price
    • Earnings manipulation: Managers may withhold bad news or use aggressive accounting to inflate share price temporarily
    • Market-wide movements: Stock options in a bull market reward managers regardless of their own performance
    • Risk aversion persists: Managers may fear large personal losses from share price falls
    Conclusion: Stock-price compensation significantly reduces — but does not eliminate — agency problems. A combination of monitoring + well-designed compensation is needed. No single mechanism is sufficient. Key: structure pay around long-term performance to limit gaming.
    Is Fixed Salary a Solution? (Specimen Essay Question)
    Answer: No. A fixed salary creates all 5 agency problems listed above. With no performance link, managers have no incentive to maximise shareholder value. Fixed salary is the root cause of the principal-agent problem, not a solution.
    • Reduced effort — no reward for working harder
    • Perks — no cost to manager for consuming private benefits
    • Overinvestment — empire building carries no personal cost
    • Risk aversion — manager bears risk of failure personally
    • Short-termism — career concerns dominate

    The solution requires output-based compensation tied to stock price, ideally over the long term, combined with monitoring.

    Ethics & the "Honest Stock Price"
    • Good governance and incentive structures discourage unethical behaviour (e.g. artificially inflating stock prices through earnings manipulation)
    • "Honest stock price" principle: Thoughtful shareholders and financial managers don't want the maximum possible stock price — they want the maximum honest stock price. The distinction matters: inflated prices through manipulation will eventually correct, destroying more value than was gained
    • Reputation effects: unethical behaviour destroys long-term value even if it creates short-term gains
    • Links to agency problem: incentive schemes must be designed carefully so managers are rewarded for genuine value creation, not for gaming metrics

    Topic 2 — Investment Decisions
    Present Value (single)
    PV = C_t / (1 + r)^t

    Present Value (multiple periods)
    PV = C₁/(1+r) + C₂/(1+r)² + … + C_T/(1+r)^T

    Net Present Value
    NPV = Σ[C_t/(1+r_t)^t] − Q₀ − Σ[Q_t/(1+r_t)^t]

    Rate of Return
    RoR = (C − Q) / Q

    IRR (find rate where NPV = 0)
    0 = Σ[C_t/(1+IRR)^t] − Q

    Profitability Index
    PI = NPV / Q (initial investment)

    Accounting/Book Rate of Return
    ARR = Average Profits / Average Assets
    Topic 3 — Corporate Financing
    Debt Ratio
    Debt Ratio = Long-term Debt / (Long-term Debt + Equity)
    Topic 4 — Equity Financing
    Cost of underpricing
    Cost = Shares sold at IPO × (End-of-day price − IPO price)

    Ex-rights price
    Ex-rights price = (N × Old price + n × Issue price) / (N + n)
    N = old shares, n = new shares

    Value of a right (rights-on)
    = (Rights-on price − Issue price) / (Rights per new share + 1)

    Value of a right (ex-rights)
    = (Ex-rights price − Issue price) / Rights per new share
    Topic 5 — Debt Policy
    Firm Value
    V = D + E

    Return on assets (rA)
    r_A = Expected Operating Income / (D + E)

    Weighted average return (rA)
    r_A = [E/(E+D)] × r_E + [D/(E+D)] × r_D

    MM Proposition II — Cost of Equity
    r_E = r_A + (r_A − r_D) × D/E

    WACC (no taxes)
    WACC = [E/(E+D)] × r_E + [D/(E+D)] × r_D = r_A (constant)

    WACC (with corporate taxes)
    WACC_after-tax = [E/(E+D)] × r_E + [D/(E+D)] × r_D × (1 − T_C)

    Tax Shield (annual)
    Tax Shield = T_C × r_D × D

    EPS after leverage
    EPS = (Operating Income − Interest) / Shares outstanding

    Shares repurchased
    Shares repurchased = Repurchase amount / Share price

    Perpetuity: Cost of equity from EPS
    r_equity = EPS / Share price (when earnings = perpetual dividend)
